What Are Common Grammar Mistakes?

Common grammar mistakes are recurring errors in sentence structure, word choice, punctuation, and usage that make writing harder to read and less credible. They're not random — they follow patterns, which means they're learnable, fixable, and preventable.

Most mistakes fall into four clean categories:

  • Punctuation errors (comma splices, apostrophe misuse)
  • Grammar errors (subject-verb agreement, dangling modifiers)
  • Word choice errors (homophones, confusable pairs)
  • Style errors (passive voice, parallel structure)

Let's go through each one in a way that actually sticks.

Punctuation Mistakes Writers Keep Making

Comma Splice

A comma splice joins two complete sentences with just a comma. It feels natural when you write it — but it reads like a grammatical car crash.

❌ I finished the report, it took three hours.
✅ I finished the report. It took three hours.
✅ I finished the report; it took three hours.

How to spot it fast: If both sides of your comma can stand alone as sentences, you have a splice. Either split them or use a semicolon.

For more on fixing comma splices, check out our detailed guide on comma rules in English.

Apostrophe Misuse

Apostrophes mark possession or contractions — never plurals. This one mistake appears in professional emails, business websites, and social media posts daily.

❌ The client's are happy. The team done it's job.
✅ The clients are happy. The team did its job.

Quick rule: Its = possession (no apostrophe). It's = it is (apostrophe = contraction).

Capitalization and Spelling

These errors are smaller than punctuation mistakes, but they still shape first impressions. Random capitalization, misspelled headings, and inconsistent brand names make writing feel rushed.

❌ the Marketing team will send the Proposal on friday.
✅ The marketing team will send the proposal on Friday.

Quick rule: Capitalize proper nouns and sentence starts only. Keep headings, brand names, and date formats consistent.

Grammar Errors That Hurt Your Credibility Most

Subject-Verb Agreement Mistakes

The subject and verb must agree in number. Sounds obvious — but compound subjects and collective nouns trip up even experienced writers.

❌ The team of managers are meeting today.
✅ The team of managers is meeting today.

How to spot it fast: Ignore the phrase between subject and verb. Ask: what is the actual subject? Then match the verb to that.

Pronoun Agreement and Reference

A pronoun must clearly match the noun it replaces. If the reference is unclear, readers slow down or misread the sentence.

❌ When Sarah met Maria, she was nervous.
✅ When Sarah met Maria, Sarah was nervous.
✅ When Sarah met Maria, Maria was nervous.

Quick rule: If a sentence has two possible antecedents, rewrite it. Clarity beats cleverness every time.

Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers

A dangling modifier is a phrase that doesn't clearly attach to the right noun. The result is often unintentionally funny — or just confusing.

❌ Walking down the street, the rain started falling.
✅ Walking down the street, I noticed the rain starting to fall.

Misplaced modifiers are similar — the modifier lands too far from what it's describing.

❌ She almost drove her car to every city in Germany.
✅ She drove her car to almost every city in Germany.

Most articles miss this: The fix isn't just moving words around — it's asking "who is doing the action?" and making that the subject right after the modifier.

Run-On Sentences

A run-on sentence jams two or more independent clauses together without proper punctuation. It exhausts the reader before they reach your point.

❌ The proposal looks good we should send it now the client is waiting.
✅ The proposal looks good. We should send it now — the client is waiting.

How to fix it: Read aloud. Where do you naturally pause or breathe? That's where punctuation belongs.

Sentence Fragments

A fragment is an incomplete thought masquerading as a sentence. It lacks a subject, a verb, or both.

❌ Because the deadline was moved.
✅ Because the deadline was moved, we had to rework the plan.

Word Choice Errors: The Confusable Pairs

These are the mistakes most grammar tools miss — because the word is spelled correctly. It's just the wrong word.

Your vs. You're

Your = belonging to you / You're = you are

❌ Your going to love this.
✅ You're going to love this.

Substitution test: Replace with "you are." If it makes sense, use you're.

There / Their / They're

There = a place / Their = belonging to them / They're = they are

❌ Their going to there house with they're friends.
✅ They're going to their house with their friends.

Affect vs. Effect

Affect = verb (to influence) / Effect = noun (the result)

❌ The change effected our results. The affect was noticeable.
✅ The change affected our results. The effect was noticeable.

Memory trick: Affect is the Action. Effect is the End result.

Then vs. Than

Then = time sequence / Than = comparison

❌ She writes better then he, then she edits.
✅ She writes better than he, then she edits.

Fewer vs. Less

Fewer = countable things / Less = uncountable amounts

❌ Less people showed up. Use fewer sugar.
✅ Fewer people showed up. Use less sugar.

Who vs. Whom

Who = subject / Whom = object

❌ Whom called you yesterday?
✅ Who called you yesterday?
❌ To who should I send this?
✅ To whom should I send this?

Quick rule: If you can replace it with he or she, use who. If you can replace it with him or her, use whom.

Grammar Mistakes in Emails and Business Writing

Here's where stakes are highest — and where I've seen the most damage. In professional writing, these mistakes signal sloppiness to clients, editors, and employers:

  • Could of / Could have — "Could of" doesn't exist in English. You want could have.
    ❌ I could of sent it earlier.
    ✅ I could have sent it earlier.
  • Passive voice overuse — Passive voice isn't wrong, but overusing it makes writing feel evasive.
    ❌ Mistakes were made.
    ✅ We made mistakes.
  • Parallel structure in lists — Every item in a list should follow the same grammatical form.
    ❌ The job requires writing reports, to manage teams, and budgets.
    ✅ The job requires writing reports, managing teams, and handling budgets.

Articles: A, An, The

Article usage is one of the most common issues for non-native writers and one of the easiest ways to make copy sound off.

❌ I need laptop for meeting.
✅ I need a laptop for the meeting.

Quick rule: Use a/an for general items, and the when the reader already knows the exact thing.

Readability and Tone

Grammar isn't only about correctness — it's about making the sentence easy to process. Long, tangled sentences, stacked modifiers, and awkward transitions can make even correct writing feel heavy.

Watch for:

  • Sentences that try to do too much.
  • Repeated words close together.
  • Unclear references after pronouns like it, this, or they.
  • Bullets that mix sentence forms.

Better writing feels clean, direct, and hard to misunderstand.

A Simple Proofreading System That Works

After years of editing my own writing and reviewing others', this is the workflow I use:

  1. Read aloud — Your ear catches what your eye skips.
  2. Read backwards (sentence by sentence) — Forces you to evaluate each sentence in isolation.
  3. Check your confusable pairs last — Do a quick find for: your/you're, its/it's, affect/effect, then/than, fewer/less.
  4. Check agreement next — Scan for subject-verb mismatches, pronoun reference issues, and parallel structure.
  5. Use a grammar tool as a second pass — Not a first one. Tools catch surface errors, but they miss context-based mistakes.

The goal isn't perfect grammar. The goal is writing that reads clean and earns trust.

Quick Reference Guide

If you only remember a few rules, make them these:

  • Your / You're: Your shows possession; you're means you are.
  • Its / It's: Its shows possession; it's means it is.
  • Then / Than: Then is time; than is comparison.
  • Affect / Effect: Affect is usually a verb; effect is usually a noun.
  • Fewer / Less: Fewer for countable things; less for uncountable amounts.
  • Comma splice: Two complete sentences need a period, semicolon, or conjunction.
  • Modifiers: Put descriptive phrases next to the word they describe.
  • Fragments: Make sure every sentence has a complete thought.

FAQ: Common Grammar Mistakes

Which grammar mistakes hurt professional credibility the most?

Subject-verb disagreement, misused apostrophes, and wrong homophones (your vs. you're) are the most damaging. They appear in headlines, email subject lines, and opening sentences — exactly where first impressions form. One mistake in a visible place signals carelessness throughout.

What are the easiest grammar mistakes to self-edit?

Confusable word pairs are the easiest to fix once you know the rules. Run a quick find-and-replace for your, its, affect, then, fewer and apply the substitution tests described above. These take under two minutes and catch a surprising number of errors.

What grammar mistakes are most common in website and email copy?

Comma splices, passive voice, and apostrophe errors dominate website copy. In emails, your/you're swaps and run-on sentences are the most frequent. Both contexts demand clean, credible writing — because readers judge brands by how they write.

What grammar mistakes do AI writing tools still miss?

Most AI tools miss context-dependent errors — particularly dangling modifiers, subtle subject-verb disagreements with collective nouns, and misused homophones in complex sentences. They also struggle with parallel structure in bulleted lists. Always proofread AI-generated content manually.

What should non-native English writers watch for most?

Article usage (a, an, the), preposition choices, and subject-verb agreement are the biggest challenges. False friends — words that look similar to words in another language but mean something different — also cause quiet errors that native speakers miss on first read.

Grammar isn't about following rigid rules for the sake of it. It's about writing clearly enough that your ideas — not your errors — are what people remember. Fix the ten mistakes in this guide, and you'll be ahead of ninety percent of writers publishing online.
