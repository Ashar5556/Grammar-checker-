What Is a Dependent Clause? Types, Examples & Rules

📅 November 5, 2026 ⏱️ 16 min read 📝 3400+ words ✍️ Grammar Guide
✨ FOUNDER OF GRAMMARIFY ✨ Written by Ashar

There's a sentence structure error I see constantly in student writing, business emails, and even published articles. It looks like a sentence. It has a subject. It has a verb. But something about it feels unfinished — because it is.

"Because the deadline was moved."

That's a dependent clause written as if it were a complete sentence. Also called a subordinate clause, it's one of the most misunderstood structures in English grammar — and one of the most important to get right.

What Is a Dependent Clause?

📌 Dependent Clause Definition

A dependent clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a predicate but cannot stand alone as a complete sentence because it does not express a complete thought.

It depends on an independent (main) clause to make sense. Remove the main clause, and what's left is a fragment — grammatically incomplete, even if it contains a subject and verb.

"Although she prepared thoroughly" — dependent clause. Something is missing. What happened although she prepared thoroughly?
"Although she prepared thoroughly, the interview didn't go as planned." — the main clause completes it. ✅

Dependent Clause vs. Subordinate Clause: Are They the Same?

Yes — completely. "Subordinate clause" is the formal grammatical term; "dependent clause" is the more commonly used equivalent in everyday grammar instruction. Both refer to the same structure: a clause that is grammatically subordinate to a main clause and cannot function independently.

You'll see both terms used interchangeably across grammar textbooks, style guides, and editing resources. Knowing they mean the same thing prevents confusion when you move between different grammar systems or educational contexts.

Dependent Clause vs. Phrase vs. Fragment

UnitSubjectFinite VerbComplete ThoughtExample
Dependent clauseYesYesNoBecause she resigned.
Independent clauseYesYesYesShe resigned.
PhraseNo or partialNoNoResigning yesterday.
FragmentSometimesSometimesNoAfter the meeting.

If a unit has a subject and finite verb but still feels unfinished, check for a subordinator or relative pronoun at the front — that's usually the culprit.

The Finite Verb Requirement

A clause needs a finite verb — a conjugated verb that shows tense and agrees with its subject.

✅ Because she writes. — finite verb, dependent clause.
❌ To write reports. — infinitive, not a clause.
❌ Writing reports daily. — participle phrase, not a clause.

A finite verb anchors the clause. Without one, you have a phrase or fragment — not a subordinate clause.

Dependent Clause Examples

The best way to internalize this structure is to see it in action across all three clause types.

Adverbial Clause Examples

"Because the power went out, the meeting was cancelled."
"She stayed late until the report was finished."
"Although the budget was tight, the project succeeded."

Adjective (Relative) Clause Examples

"The candidate who scored highest was offered the role."
"The document, which had been revised three times, was finally approved."
"I returned the book that I borrowed last month."

Noun Clause Examples

"What she said surprised the entire team." (subject)
"He didn't know whether the shipment had arrived." (object)
"The problem is that no one communicated the change." (complement)

Types of Dependent Clauses

Most guides mention types in passing. Here's a clear breakdown that actually helps with identification and punctuation.

Adverbial Clauses

Adverbial clauses modify the main verb and are introduced by subordinating conjunctions: because, although, when, while, since, if, unless, before, after, until, even though.

They answer: When? Why? How? Under what condition?

"When the results came in, the team celebrated."

Position matters for punctuation: adverbial clauses at the front of a sentence take a comma; at the end, they typically don't.

Mid-sentence interruption:

"The team, because the deadline was tight, worked overtime."

Here the clause sits between the subject and verb, set off by commas on both sides — common in business and academic writing when you want to add context without starting a new sentence.

Adjective (Relative) Clauses

Also called relative clauses, these modify nouns and are introduced by relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that.

They answer: Which one? What kind?

"The manager who approved the budget left the company."

Restrictive vs. Nonrestrictive Relative Clauses: The Comma Rule

Punctuation depends on whether the relative clause is essential (restrictive) or nonessential (nonrestrictive) to the sentence's meaning. This is the question most writers have immediately after learning what relative clauses are.

Restrictive (essential) — no commas: The clause identifies which specific noun is meant. Removing it changes the meaning.

"The report that the analyst submitted was inconclusive." → Without it: "The report was inconclusive." — which report? Unclear.

Nonrestrictive (nonessential) — commas required: The clause adds extra information about an already-identified noun. Removing it leaves the core meaning intact.

"The annual report, which the analyst submitted Friday, was inconclusive." → Without it: "The annual report was inconclusive." — still clear.

Minimal pair showing how the comma changes meaning:

"The manager who approved the budget left." — one specific manager (the one who approved it)
"The manager, who approved the budget, left." — one manager, with the approval as extra information

American English conventions (that vs. which vs. who):

  • That for restrictive clauses: "The file that you deleted is gone."
  • Which for nonrestrictive clauses: "The file, which you deleted, is gone."
  • Who (not that) for people in formal writing: "The analyst who submitted the report" ✅

Noun Clauses

Noun clauses function as nouns — they can be subjects, direct objects, subject complements, or objects of prepositions. Common introducers include that, what, whatever, who, whoever, whether, how.

Four grammatical functions:

FunctionExample
Subject"What she said surprised the entire team."
Direct object"He didn't know whether the shipment had arrived."
Subject complement"The problem is that no one communicated the change."
Object of preposition"She was surprised by what he proposed."

The that-omission rule: in object position, that can often be dropped without changing meaning. "She knew (that) he was right." — both versions are correct. In subject position, that cannot be dropped: "That he lied was clear." ✅ | "He lied was clear." ❌

The whether vs. if distinction: in formal writing, use whether for embedded questions, especially before infinitives and at the start of a sentence. "I wonder whether he'll call." (formal) vs. "I wonder if he'll call." (informal — both acceptable)

Agreement with noun clause subjects: noun clauses functioning as subjects take a singular verb. "What she needs is better resources." — is, not are.

Diagnostic trick: Replace the clause with it or something. If the sentence still works grammatically, you're looking at a noun clause.

Reduced Dependent Clauses

Professional and literary prose frequently uses reduced subordinate clauses — dependent clauses from which the subject and auxiliary verb have been deleted because they're recoverable from context. Writers who know that subordinate clauses need a subject and predicate will misclassify these as phrases.

Reduced Adverbial Clauses

"Although exhausted, she continued working." Full form: "Although she was exhausted, she continued working."
"While driving to the office, he took the call." Full form: "While he was driving to the office, he took the call."

Comma rule: reduced adverbial clauses follow the same position-based rule as full ones — comma after if fronted, no comma if following the main clause.

Reduced Relative Clauses

"The report submitted on Friday was approved." Full form: "The report that was submitted on Friday was approved."
"The analysts hired last year all received promotions." Full form: "The analysts who were hired last year all received promotions."

The method: reconstruct the full form by restoring the subject and auxiliary verb. If the result is a recognizable subordinate clause, the reduced form follows the same clause rules — not phrase rules.

Embedded Dependent Clauses: Clauses Inside Clauses

Academic, legal, and journalistic writing regularly uses embedded subordinate clauses — clauses nested inside other clauses. Finding the main clause requires stripping the embedded layers.

"She knew that what he said was false."
  • Inner noun clause: what he said (subject of the embedded clause)
  • Outer noun clause: that what he said was false (direct object)
  • Main clause: She knew...
"The analyst who reviewed the data that was flagged resigned."
  • Inner relative clause: that was flagged (modifies data)
  • Outer relative clause: who reviewed the data that was flagged (modifies analyst)
  • Main clause: The analyst... resigned.

The stripping method: remove embedded clauses from the inside out until a complete, independent thought remains. That's your main clause. Agreement, punctuation, and clause-type decisions should all reference the main clause once it's identified.

Dependent Clauses and Subject-Verb Agreement

Clause structure directly affects verb agreement — a connection most grammar guides leave unaddressed.

  • Relative clause interrupting subject and verb: "The data that the analysts collected were inconclusive." The relative clause (that the analysts collected) interrupts the path from data to were. The verb agrees with data, not with analysts.
  • Noun clause as subject: "What she needs is better resources." The noun clause is the subject — treated as singular. Verb: is, not are.
  • "One of the [plural noun] who/that" constructions: "She is one of the analysts who were promoted." The relative clause (who were promoted) modifies analysts (plural), not one. Verb inside the relative clause: were, not was.

These are among the most common errors in academic writing, and they trace directly to misidentifying which clause governs which verb.

How to Identify a Dependent Clause

Here's the three-step method I use — and teach — for any clause identification question.

  1. Find the subject and predicate. The clause must have both. If either is missing, you may be looking at a phrase, not a clause.
  2. Look for a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun. Words like because, although, when, since, if, who, which, that are the clearest markers. Their presence almost always signals a subordinate clause.
  3. Test for completeness. Read the clause in isolation. Does it express a full, finished thought? If it leaves you waiting for more — it's dependent.
"Since he submitted early" — has a subject (he) and a predicate (submitted early), but since creates an expectation. It's a subordinate clause.

Knowing which marker you're dealing with tells you the clause's role — and its punctuation behavior.

Comma Rules for Dependent Clauses

The comma rule is position-based, and it's one of the most consistently misapplied rules in everyday writing.

Subordinate clause first → comma required:

"Because the meeting ran long, we missed the train." ✅

Independent clause first → no comma (typically):

"We missed the train because the meeting ran long." ✅

The comma signals where the introductory clause ends and the main clause begins. When the main clause opens the sentence, that signal isn't needed — the reader flows naturally into the subordinate clause.

Exception — extreme contrast:

"She never missed a deadline, even though she was overloaded." When the dependent clause adds strong contrast or emphasis, a comma may be used even when it follows the main clause.

Independent Clause vs. Dependent Clause: What's the Difference?

FeatureIndependent ClauseDependent Clause
Has a subjectYesYes
Has a predicateYesYes
Expresses a complete thoughtYesNo
Can stand alone as a sentenceYesNo
Common opening wordsNouns, pronounsbecause, although, who, which, that, when, if

The simplest diagnostic: Can it stand alone? If yes — independent. If no — dependent.

One realization I had after years of editing: the subject-verb test alone is not enough. I used to mark any clause with a subject and verb as complete. It isn't always. "Although he tried" has both — and it's still a fragment without a main clause attached.

A Note for ESL Writers

Subordinate clause errors appear at higher rates in ESL writing for specific, predictable reasons — not because of grammatical misunderstanding, but because of L1 transfer: applying the clause conventions of one language to English.

Three common transfer patterns:

  • Pro-drop languages (Spanish, Italian, Arabic, Hindi): clause subjects are dropped when recoverable from context. "Because arrived late" is complete in Spanish (Porque llegó tarde); in English it's a fragment. Make the subject visible: "Because she arrived late."
  • Head-final languages (Japanese, Korean, Turkish): subordination markers appear at the end of a clause, not the beginning. English subordinators open the clause — the opposite convention.
  • Arabic and Chinese clause-chaining: both languages use comma-connected clause chains where English requires stronger separation (period, semicolon, or conjunction). These chains, carried into English, produce run-ons and comma splices rather than properly marked subordination.
💡 The practical rule:

in English, the subordinating word appears at the very start of the dependent clause and is non-negotiable. It cannot be dropped, moved to the end, or replaced by a comma.

Dependent Clauses in Legal and Contractual Writing

In legal and contractual language, subordinate clause identification carries professional consequences — not just grammatical ones.

Conditional clauses define the scope of obligations: "The contractor shall complete Phase One unless the client requests a scope change." The conditional clause (unless the client requests a scope change) limits the obligation. Misidentifying it as an independent clause — or punctuating it as one — can create ambiguity about whether the condition is binding.

Fragment errors in contracts create interpretive risk: "Provided that all deliverables meet the agreed specifications." written as a standalone sentence leaves the obligation it's meant to qualify unattached. Courts have ruled on whether such constructions create enforceable conditions or mere recitals.

⚠️ The standard: in legal drafting, every conditional and relative subordinate clause must be explicitly attached to the main obligation clause it qualifies. When in doubt, restructure into two clear sentences rather than rely on a complex embedded structure.

Dependent Clause Fragment Errors in AI-Generated Content

AI language models produce subordinate clause fragments at a meaningful rate — particularly in list expansions, explanatory passages, and narrative transitions where the model generates clause-like structures that appear complete but lack a main clause.

Common AI fragment patterns:

"Because the update was deployed automatically." — adverbial clause written as a sentence
"Which resulted in a significant improvement in performance." — relative clause without an antecedent sentence
"Although users reported the issue multiple times." — subordinate clause with no resolution

Why AI produces them: language models optimize for surface-level fluency. A subordinate clause fragment often reads smoothly in context — especially when the implied main clause is present in the surrounding paragraph — so the model generates it without flagging the structural error.

How to audit AI drafts:

  • Scan for every sentence beginning with because, although, when, which, who, that, since — these are high-probability fragment locations
  • Apply Step 3 of the identification method: read the clause in isolation and ask whether it expresses a complete thought
  • Check for relative clause fragments (which resulted in...) that appear to modify a noun in the previous sentence but are punctuated as standalone sentences

Grammar checkers catch only a fraction of AI-generated fragment errors. Manual clause-level review remains the most reliable audit method.

Dependent Clauses in a Complex Sentence

When a subordinate clause is attached to a main clause, the result is a complex sentence — one of the most common and effective sentence types in English.

"Although the deadline was tight, the team delivered a strong result."

One independent clause + one subordinate clause = one complex sentence. This structure gives writers a way to show which idea is primary (the main clause) and which provides context (the subordinate clause). For more on how these structures scale up, see independent and dependent clauses.

Sentence Type Taxonomy

Sentence TypeStructureExample
SimpleOne independent clauseShe resigned.
CompoundTwo+ independent clausesShe resigned, and he took over.
ComplexOne independent + one+ dependent clauseBecause she resigned, he took over.
Compound-complexTwo+ independent + one+ dependent clauseBecause she resigned, he took over, and the team adjusted.

Knowing these structures helps you diagnose punctuation problems faster — most errors happen at the seams between clauses.

Multiple Dependent Clauses in One Sentence

"Although she was tired, she stayed until the work was done because the client needed it."
  • Dependent clause 1: Although she was tired — introductory, comma required.
  • Independent clause: she stayed until the work was done — main clause.
  • Dependent clause 2: because the client needed it — final position, no comma needed.

Layered subordinate clauses are common in academic and professional writing. The key is tracking each clause's position to apply the right punctuation.

Common Mistakes With Dependent Clauses

  • Fragment error: Writing a subordinate clause as a full sentence.
    ❌ "Because the deadline moved."
    ✅ Attach it to a main clause: "Because the deadline moved, we revised the plan."
  • Comma splice: Joining two independent clauses with only a comma.
    ❌ "She called, he didn't answer."
    ✅ Add a conjunction or semicolon.
  • Missing comma after introductory clause:
    ❌ "Because the meeting ran long we missed the train."
    ✅ "Because the meeting ran long, we missed the train."
  • Unnecessary comma before a final subordinate clause:
    ❌ "She stayed late, because the report wasn't finished."
    ✅ Remove the comma unless adding deliberate contrast.
  • Misidentifying noun clauses as fragments:
    ❌ Treating "What she said" as a fragment when it functions as a subject
    ✅ "What she said surprised everyone."
  • Misclassifying reduced clauses as phrases:
    ❌ Treating "Although exhausted" as a participial phrase
    ✅ It's a reduced adverbial clause; comma rules apply.
  • Agreement errors caused by embedded clauses:
    ❌ "The data that the analysts collected was..." (if treating analysts as the subject)
    ✅ Identify the true subject (data) before choosing the verb form.

Clause Ambiguity: When Punctuation Changes Meaning

"Let's eat, Grandma." vs. "Let's eat Grandma."

A comma can completely change who's being addressed — and what's happening.

"The manager who approved the budget left." vs. "The manager, who approved the budget, left."
  • First version: only one specific manager — the one who approved the budget.
  • Second version: one manager, with the budget approval as extra information.

Punctuation isn't decoration — it's meaning.

FAQ

What is the difference between a dependent clause and a subordinate clause?

They are the same thing — two names for the same grammatical structure. "Subordinate clause" is the formal term used in most grammar and linguistics texts; "dependent clause" is the term more commonly used in everyday writing instruction. Both describe a clause that has a subject and finite verb but cannot stand alone as a sentence because it is introduced by a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun that makes it grammatically incomplete without a main clause.

What are the three types of dependent clauses?

The three types are: (1) Adverbial clauses, which modify the main verb and answer when, why, how, or under what condition — introduced by subordinating conjunctions like because, although, when, if; (2) Adjective (relative) clauses, which modify nouns and are introduced by relative pronouns like who, which, that; and (3) Noun clauses, which function as subjects, direct objects, subject complements, or objects of prepositions — introduced by that, what, whether, how. Each type follows different comma rules based on its position and whether it is essential to the sentence's meaning.

Can a dependent clause be a subject?

Yes — when it is a noun clause. "What she said surprised the entire team" contains a noun clause (What she said) functioning as the sentence's subject. Noun clause subjects take a singular verb regardless of their internal complexity: "What she needs is better resources" — the verb is is, not are. This is one of the most frequently misidentified structures in English because noun clauses don't "feel" dependent the way adverbial clauses do — yet they still require a main clause to anchor the sentence.

How do you fix a dependent clause written as a sentence fragment?

Two options: (1) Attach it to an adjacent main clause — the cleanest solution in most cases. "Because the deadline moved." → "Because the deadline moved, we revised the plan." (2) Remove the subordinating conjunction — this converts the clause into an independent one. "Because the deadline moved" → "The deadline moved." Grammar checkers miss fragment errors frequently in longer sentences because a subject and verb are present. The reliable test is Step 3: read the clause in isolation and ask whether it expresses a complete, finished thought.

When do you use a comma with a dependent clause?

Position determines the comma. When a subordinate clause opens the sentence — before the main clause — use a comma after it: "Although it was late, she kept working." When the main clause comes first and the subordinate clause follows, a comma is typically not used: "She kept working although it was late." The exception is a strong contrast or emphatic addition at the end, where a comma is optional and stylistic. Clause position, not the length or content of the clause, is the governing rule.

What is a reduced dependent clause, and how is it different from a phrase?

A reduced subordinate clause is a dependent clause from which the subject and auxiliary verb have been deleted because they are recoverable from context. "Although exhausted, she continued" is a reduced adverbial clause — its full form is "Although she was exhausted." A phrase, by contrast, never had a finite subject-verb pair to begin with. The practical difference: reduced clauses follow the same comma rules as their full forms (comma after if fronted), and their presence signals a clause-level grammatical relationship, not just a descriptive modifier.

Is a relative clause always a dependent clause?

Yes. A relative clause is a specific type of subordinate clause that modifies a noun and is introduced by a relative pronoun (who, whom, whose, which, that). Like all dependent clauses, it cannot stand alone as a sentence. Whether it takes commas depends on whether it is restrictive (essential — no commas) or nonrestrictive (nonessential — commas required). "The analyst who submitted the report" is restrictive; "The analyst, who submitted the report," is nonrestrictive. The comma changes which interpretation the reader receives.

How do you identify a dependent clause in a long or complex sentence?

Use the stripping method: temporarily remove all embedded relative clauses (who..., which..., that...) and all adverbial clauses (because..., although..., when...) from the sentence. What remains should be the main clause — a complete independent thought. If what remains doesn't express a full thought, the main clause is buried inside the embedding and needs to be re-identified. Once the main clause is located, apply the three-step test to each remaining clause: subject present? Finite verb present? Complete thought without the main clause? If the third answer is no, it's a dependent clause.

The dependent clause is the grammar structure that shows relationship — it tells readers what's background, what's context, what's condition. An independent clause states. A subordinate clause situates. Used well together, they give your writing both clarity and depth. That's not a grammar technicality. That's the difference between writing that informs and writing that actually communicates.
