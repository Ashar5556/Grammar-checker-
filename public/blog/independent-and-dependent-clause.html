Independent vs Dependent Clause: Definitions & Rules

📅 October 23, 2026 ⏱️ 16 min read 📝 3000+ words ✍️ Grammar Guide
✨ FOUNDER OF GRAMMARIFY ✨ Written by Ashar

When I first started editing other people's writing, I kept flagging sentences that felt "off" without being able to explain why. The sentences had subjects. They had verbs. But something about their structure made them incomplete — or overcrowded.

The answer, almost every time, came down to clause type. Once I understood the difference between a clause that can stand alone and one that can't, everything else in sentence structure clicked into place.

Independent Clause vs. Dependent Clause: What's the Difference?

This is the question most writers are actually asking, even when they search for definitions. So let's answer it directly before anything else.

📌 Quick Definition

Independent clause = subject + verb + complete thought (can stand alone)

Dependent clause = subject + verb + incomplete thought (needs a main clause)

Clause vs. Phrase: A Foundational Distinction

Before anything else, one distinction needs to be clear — because confusing these two units causes misidentification at every step that follows.

A clause has both a subject and a verb. A phrase does not — or if it contains a verb form, that verb form is not functioning as the clause's main predicate.

UnitExampleSubject + Verb?Complete?
PhraseRunning to catch the trainNo finite subjectNo
Dependent clauseWhen she ran to catch the trainYes (she ran)No — needs more
Independent clauseShe ran to catch the trainYes (she ran)Yes

The practical rule: if you can find a subject performing a finite verb action, you have a clause. If not — if the verb is in participial (running), infinitive (to run), or gerund (running as a noun) form without a subject — you have a phrase.

This distinction is what makes Step 1 of the identification method ("find the subject and verb") reliable rather than misleading.

What Is an Independent Clause?

An independent clause — also called a main clause — is a group of words that contains a subject and a verb and expresses a complete thought. It can stand alone as a sentence.

"The report was finished." Subject: The report. Verb: was finished. Complete thought: yes. Independent.

What Is a Dependent Clause?

A dependent clause — also called a subordinate clause — also has a subject and a verb, but it does not express a complete thought on its own. It depends on an independent clause to make sense.

"Because the report was finished." Same subject and verb. But the word because creates an expectation the clause doesn't fulfill. What happened because the report was finished? The clause is incomplete. It needs more.

The word that opens a dependent clause — because, although, when, since, if, unless, while, after, before — is called a subordinating conjunction. It's the clearest signal that what follows is a subordinate clause, not a standalone sentence.

Common Subordinating Conjunctions and Relative Pronouns

Spotting these words at the start of a clause is the fastest way to identify a subordinate clause in practice.

Subordinating Conjunctions

  • Time: when, after, before, while, until, since
  • Cause / Reason: because, since, as
  • Contrast: although, even though, whereas
  • Condition: if, unless, whether

Relative Pronouns (often start adjective clauses)

who, whom, whose, which, that

If a clause begins with one of these and feels incomplete, it's almost certainly dependent.

How to Identify Independent and Dependent Clauses

Here's a four-step method you can apply to any clause in any sentence.

  1. Find the subject and verb.
  2. Ask: does this group of words express a complete thought?
  3. Check if the clause starts with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun (who, which, that, whom, whose).
  4. If it starts with a subordinator and feels incomplete — it's dependent. If it expresses a full thought and stands on its own — it's independent.

Most writers skip Step 3. That's the step that catches the tricky cases — clauses that have subjects and verbs but are still fragments because of how they open.

"Which the committee approved."

Has a verb. Has a noun. But starts with which (a relative pronoun). Dependent clause — incomplete without a main clause before it.

Quick Checklist

  • Does it have a subject and verb?
  • Does it express a complete thought?
  • Does it start with a subordinator or relative pronoun?
  • Can it stand alone as a sentence?

If the answer to the last two is "no," you're looking at a dependent clause.

Independent and Dependent Clauses: Examples Side by Side

Seeing them together is the fastest way to internalize the difference.

Independent Clause Examples

"She submitted the application." ✅ Complete. Standalone.
"The server crashed." ✅ Complete. Standalone.
"He understood the instructions." ✅ Complete. Standalone.

Dependent Clause Examples

"After she submitted the application..." ❌ Incomplete. Waiting for more.
"When the server crashed..." ❌ Incomplete. What happened when?
"Although he understood the instructions..." ❌ Incomplete. What's the contrast?

Every subordinate clause above is a sentence fragment on its own. Attaching it to a main clause completes the meaning.

Sentence Fragments: When a Dependent Clause Stands Alone

This is the most common practical consequence of misidentifying clause type — and the article's Common Mistakes bullet doesn't do it justice. A dependent clause fragment looks like a sentence: it has a subject, a verb, and punctuation. But the subordinating word makes it incomplete.

Adverbial clause fragment:

"Although she prepared carefully." ❌ → What happened? The main clause is missing.
"Although she prepared carefully, the interview was difficult." ✅

Relative clause fragment:

"Which the committee had reviewed twice." ❌ → Modifies what noun?
"The proposal, which the committee had reviewed twice, was rejected." ✅

Noun clause fragment:

"That the deadline had passed." ❌ → Subject or object of what?
"She realized that the deadline had passed." ✅

Two fixes for any fragment:

  • Attach it to an adjacent sentence — the cleanest solution in most cases
  • Remove the subordinating conjunction — converts the dependent clause into an independent one: "Although she prepared carefully" → "She prepared carefully."

Grammar checkers miss fragment errors frequently in longer sentences because the subject-verb pair is present. Manual clause-type identification is more reliable.

Types of Dependent Clauses: Not All Subordinate Clauses Work the Same Way

Most guides stop at the basic definition. But subordinate clauses come in three distinct types, and each one functions differently in a sentence.

Adverbial Clauses

Modify verbs and answer questions like when, why, how, under what condition. Introduced by subordinating conjunctions.

"She called before the meeting started."

Adjective Clauses (Relative Clauses)

Modify nouns and are introduced by relative pronouns: who, which, that, whom, whose.

"The candidate who interviewed first got the role."

Noun Clauses

Function as nouns — they can act as subjects, direct objects, subject complements, or objects of prepositions. This is the most structurally complex type and the most frequently misidentified.

FunctionExample
Subject"What she said surprised everyone."
Direct object"I know that he lied."
Subject complement"The problem is that we ran out of time."
Object of preposition"She was interested in what he proposed."

Punctuation note for noun clauses: when a noun clause functions as a direct object, no comma precedes it — "She said that the meeting was canceled" takes no comma before that. When the noun clause is the subject, watch for subject-verb agreement: "What she needs is better resources" — the noun clause subject takes a singular verb.

Understanding which type of dependent clause you're working with matters for punctuation. Adjective clauses that are nonessential get commas; essential ones don't. Adverbial clauses at the front of a sentence get a comma; at the end, typically not.

Restrictive vs. Nonrestrictive Relative Clauses: The Comma Rule That Changes Meaning

The adjective clause section needs more than one sentence on punctuation — because this is one of the most searched and most frequently misapplied rules in English grammar.

Restrictive (essential) relative clause: identifies which specific noun is meant. No commas. Removing it changes the sentence's meaning.

"The report that the analyst submitted was inconclusive."

Without the clause: "The report was inconclusive." — which report? Unclear.

Nonrestrictive (nonessential) relative clause: adds extra information about an already-identified noun. Commas required. Removing it leaves the core meaning intact.

"The annual report, which the analyst submitted on Friday, was inconclusive."

Without the clause: "The annual report was inconclusive." — still clear.

That vs. Which (US vs. UK Convention)

  • American English (CMOS, AP): use that for restrictive clauses, which for nonrestrictive clauses: "The file that you deleted" vs. "The file, which you deleted,"
  • British English: which is acceptable in both roles; punctuation carries the distinction

Who vs. That for People

In formal writing, use who (not that) when referring to people in a relative clause:

"The analyst who submitted the report..." ✅
"The analyst that submitted the report..." ❌ (acceptable in informal use; avoid in formal contexts)

Reduced and Elliptical Clauses

Professional and literary prose frequently uses reduced clauses — dependent clauses from which the subject and auxiliary verb have been deleted. Writers who know full clause rules but encounter a reduced form often misidentify it as a phrase.

Reduced Adverbial Clauses

"While driving to the office, she took the call."

Full form: "While she was driving to the office..." The subject (she) and auxiliary (was) are deleted because they're recoverable from context.

"Although exhausted, she continued working."

Full form: "Although she was exhausted..."

Comma rule: reduced adverbial clauses follow the same position-based comma rule as full ones — comma after if fronted, no comma if following the main clause.

Elliptical Clauses (Comparisons)

"She works harder than he does."

does is ellipted from the comparison clause

"He is taller than she [is]."

the verb is implied

These structures are grammatically complete as elliptical constructions; the omitted elements are recoverable. No comma is inserted to mark the omission.

Embedded Clauses: Clauses Inside Clauses

Academic writing, legal drafting, and complex journalism regularly use embedded clauses — clauses nested inside other clauses. Finding the main clause requires stripping the embedded layers.

"The report that the analyst who reviewed the data submitted was inconclusive."

Stripped down:

  • Innermost relative clause: who reviewed the data (modifies analyst)
  • Outer relative clause: that the analyst... submitted (modifies report)
  • Main clause: The report... was inconclusive

The method for embedded structures:

  1. Find the subject-verb pair that isn't inside any relative or noun clause
  2. Strip each embedded clause from the outside in until you find the main clause
  3. Verify: the remaining clause must express a complete thought and not begin with a subordinator
"She knew that what he said was false."
  • Inner noun clause: what he said (subject of the embedded clause)
  • Outer noun clause: that what he said was false (direct object)
  • Main clause: She knew...

When agreement or punctuation decisions depend on finding the main clause, this stripping method is the most reliable tool.

Clause Type and Subject-Verb Agreement

Clause structure directly affects agreement — a connection most grammar articles leave unaddressed.

  • Relative clause interrupting subject and verb: "The data that the analysts collected were inconclusive." The relative clause (that the analysts collected) interrupts the path from subject (data) to verb (were). The verb agrees with data, not with analysts.
  • Noun clause as subject: "What she needs is better resources." The noun clause What she needs is the subject — treated as singular. Verb: is.
  • "One of the [plural noun] that/who" constructions: "She is one of the analysts who were promoted." The relative clause (who were promoted) modifies analysts (plural) — not one. Verb inside the relative clause: were, not was.

These are among the most common agreement errors in academic writing, and they trace directly to misidentifying which clause governs the verb.

How Clause Types Build Sentence Structures

Combining independent and subordinate clauses creates different sentence types:

Sentence TypeStructureExample
SimpleOne independent clauseShe submitted the report.
CompoundTwo or more independent clausesShe submitted the report, and it was approved.
ComplexOne independent clause + at least one dependent clauseAlthough it was late, she submitted the report.
Compound-complexTwo or more independent clauses + at least one dependent clauseAlthough it was late, she submitted the report, and it was approved.

Understanding these patterns helps vary sentence rhythm without losing clarity.

How to Combine Independent and Dependent Clauses

Joining these two clause types is how complex sentences are built. The combination of one main clause and one subordinate clause creates a complex sentence — one of the most flexible structures in English.

When the Dependent Clause Comes First

When the subordinate clause opens the sentence, use a comma after it before the main clause begins.

"Although the deadline was tight, the team delivered on time."
"Because she prepared carefully, the presentation went smoothly."

The comma signals where the introductory clause ends and the main clause begins. Without it, the two elements blur together.

When the Independent Clause Comes First

When the main clause comes first and the dependent clause follows, no comma is typically needed.

"The team delivered on time although the deadline was tight."
"The presentation went smoothly because she prepared carefully."

The clause order reversal removes the need for a comma in most cases. The exception: when the dependent clause expresses a strong contrast, some writers and style guides allow a comma for clarity — "She liked the design, although she changed her mind later." — but this is a stylistic choice, not a hard rule.

Quick Decision Tree for Commas With Dependent Clauses

  • Dependent clause at the start? → Use a comma
  • Independent clause at the start? → Usually no comma
  • Strong contrast at the end? → Comma optional (stylistic)

I used to add commas based on where I felt a pause — which was unreliable. Following clause order is far more consistent.

Compound-Complex Sentences: Putting It All Together

The article mentions compound-complex sentences in the table but never shows one being built and punctuated. Here are two fully annotated examples.

Example 1: "Although the deadline was tight, the team delivered on time, and the client was satisfied."

  • Although the deadline was tight — dependent clause (adverbial; comma after because fronted)
  • the team delivered on time — independent clause
  • and the client was satisfied — second independent clause (comma + conjunction joining)

Example 2: "She accepted the offer that had been extended, but she negotiated the terms before signing."

  • She accepted the offer — first independent clause
  • that had been extended — dependent clause (restrictive relative; no comma — identifies which offer)
  • but she negotiated the terms before signing — second independent clause (comma + but)

Annotation habit: label each clause type before deciding on punctuation. The labels dictate the marks — not the other way around.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Treating a dependent clause as a complete sentence (fragment error)
    ❌ "Although the report was finished."
    ✅ "Although the report was finished, she sent it immediately."
  • Missing the comma after a fronted dependent clause
    ❌ "When the power went out the meeting ended."
    ✅ "When the power went out, the meeting ended."
  • Overusing subordination — creating nested, hard-to-follow sentences; rewrite as two sentences when you have more than two levels of embedding
  • Assuming all long clauses are independent — check for subordinating conjunctions before deciding
    ❌ Treating "Because she had prepared extensively for the interview" as a standalone sentence
  • Ignoring clause order when deciding on commas — position, not pause, determines the comma
  • Misidentifying reduced clauses as phrases
    ❌ Treating "Although exhausted" as a participial phrase
    ✅ It's a reduced adverbial clause
  • Agreement errors caused by embedded relative clauses
    ❌ "The data that the analysts collected was..." (if data is treated as plural)
    ✅ Identify the true subject before choosing the verb form

FAQ

What is the difference between a clause and a phrase?

A clause contains a finite subject-verb pair — a subject performing a main verb action. A phrase does not have this combination: it may contain a noun or a verb form, but not both functioning as subject and predicate. "Running to catch the train" is a participial phrase — no subject is performing running as a finite action. "When she ran to catch the train" is a dependent clause — she is the subject, ran is the finite verb. This distinction is the foundation of all clause identification.

What is the difference between an independent and a dependent clause?

An independent clause contains a subject and verb and expresses a complete thought — it can stand alone as a sentence. A dependent (or subordinate) clause also has a subject and verb, but begins with a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun, leaving the meaning incomplete. It needs a main clause to form a full sentence. The presence of a subordinating word is the most reliable marker of clause dependence.

What is a subordinate clause?

A subordinate clause is another name for a dependent clause — a clause that cannot stand alone because it is introduced by a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, if) or a relative pronoun (who, which, that). The term "subordinate" reflects its grammatical status: it is ranked below the main clause and depends on it for complete meaning. All three types — adverbial, adjective/relative, and noun clauses — are subordinate clauses.

Can a dependent clause function as a subject?

Yes — when it's a noun clause. "What she said surprised everyone" contains a noun clause (What she said) functioning as the sentence's subject. Noun clauses can also serve as direct objects ("I know that he lied"), subject complements ("The issue is that we ran out of time"), and objects of prepositions ("She was interested in what he proposed"). Subject noun clauses take singular verbs regardless of their internal complexity.

What is the comma rule for dependent clauses?

The position of the subordinate clause determines the comma. When the dependent clause comes first — before the main clause — use a comma after it: "Although it was late, she kept working." When the main clause comes first and the subordinate clause follows, a comma is typically not needed: "She kept working although it was late." The exception is a strong contrast at the end, where a comma is optional and stylistic. Clause position, not pause, is the governing rule.

What is the difference between a restrictive and nonrestrictive relative clause?

A restrictive relative clause identifies which specific noun is meant — it's essential to the sentence's meaning and takes no commas: "The report that the analyst submitted was late." A nonrestrictive relative clause adds extra information about an already-identified noun — it's removable without changing the core meaning and requires commas: "The annual report, which the analyst submitted Friday, was late." In American English, that is standard for restrictive clauses and which for nonrestrictive; British English uses which in both roles, with punctuation carrying the distinction.

What is a reduced clause, and how is it different from a phrase?

A reduced clause is a dependent clause from which the subject and auxiliary verb have been deleted because they're recoverable from context. "Although exhausted, she continued" is a reduced adverbial clause — its full form is "Although she was exhausted." A phrase, by contrast, never had a finite subject-verb pair to begin with. The practical difference matters for punctuation: reduced clauses follow the same comma rules as their full forms (comma after if fronted, no comma if following the main clause), while participial phrases have their own set of comma conventions.

Clause type is the foundation of sentence structure. Once you can reliably identify what's independent and what's subordinate, every other punctuation decision — commas, semicolons, sentence boundaries — becomes a matter of applying a clear rule rather than guessing. The clause is where the logic lives. The punctuation just shows the reader where to find it.
