Parenthetical Elements: How to Punctuate Nonessential Asides

Parenthetical Elements: How to Punctuate the Words Your Sentence Doesn't Strictly Need

Here's a sentence I once submitted to an editor that came back with a single note in the margin: "What is this doing inside these commas?"

The sentence was: "The proposal, which had been revised three times, was finally approved."

The clause was fine. My editor's issue was that I'd been inconsistently using commas, dashes, and parentheses throughout the document — sometimes for the same type of information — with no apparent logic. It looked careless. It read that way too.

That note sent me back to the fundamentals of interrupters, asides, and supplementary information in sentences. What I found was a clear system — and a surprisingly elegant one. It is as damaging to your professional credibility as any other common punctuation marks and mistakes on the page.

What Is a Parenthetical Element?

A parenthetical element is any word, phrase, or clause inserted into a sentence that adds information but is not grammatically essential to the core meaning. Remove it, and the sentence still stands — complete and accurate.

"My colleague, incidentally, has the same problem." Remove incidentally → "My colleague has the same problem." Still works. The word is parenthetical.

The term covers a wide range: transitional expressions, appositives, nonrestrictive clauses, interjections, and editorial asides. What they share is removability. What differs is how you punctuate them — and that choice carries real meaning.

Quick Definition

📌 Quick Definition

Parenthetical elements are nonessential additions to a sentence, and their punctuation tells the reader how closely to connect them to the main thought.

Common Parenthetical Types

  • Transitional words and phrases
  • Appositives
  • Nonrestrictive relative clauses
  • Interjections and asides
  • Technical notes and citations

The Four-Way Punctuation Choice: Commas, Dashes, Parentheses, or Brackets

Most guides focus on commas and leave it there. That's the gap. Because commas, parentheses, em dashes, and square brackets each signal something different — and choosing the wrong container changes how the reader weights the information.

MarkReader EffectBest Use
CommasSmooth, integratedStandard interrupters, appositives, transitional phrases
Em DashesSharp, emphaticInformation you want the reader to notice
Parentheses ( )Quiet, detachedSupplementary detail, abbreviations, citations
Square Brackets [ ]Editorial, clarifyingInsertions into quoted material, nested notes, [sic]
"The CEO — who had been in the role for less than a year — reversed the decision." The em dashes make the time detail feel significant. Swap them for commas and it reads as routine information.
"The company (founded in 1998) has expanded globally." Parentheses tuck the date away. It's available but not pushed at the reader.
💡 My bold opinion

Most writers overuse parentheses. When inserted information genuinely matters to your sentence, integrate it with commas or dashes. Reserve parentheses for details that are truly secondary — definitions, abbreviations, or citations that only some readers need.

Quick Decision Guide

  • Use commas when the aside should feel smooth and integrated.
  • Use em dashes when you want the inserted content to stand out.
  • Use parentheses when the detail is useful but clearly secondary.
  • Use square brackets when inserting editorial clarification into a quotation.

Em Dash vs. En Dash: The Distinction Writers Miss

The article uses "dashes" — but there are two, and only one belongs in parenthetical writing.

Em dash (—): the parenthetical dash. Used for interruption, emphasis, and asides. Named for its width (roughly the letter M). This is what you use when setting off an interrupter with force.

En dash (–): used for ranges (2020–2030), scores (3–1), and compound adjectives (New York–London route). Never for parenthetical asides.

Keyboard shortcuts:

  • Em dash — Mac: Option + Shift + Hyphen | Windows: Alt + 0151
  • En dash – Mac: Option + Hyphen | Windows: Alt + 0150

Spacing conventions:

  • American style (CMOS): no spaces around the em dash — like this
  • British style: spaced em dashes or spaced en dashes — like this — depending on publisher preference
💡 Pro Tip

When you see a hyphen used as a dash in prose, it's almost always an em dash that hasn't been formatted correctly. Two hyphens (--) are the plain-text substitute; proper typesetting uses the em dash character.

Square Brackets: The Fourth Parenthetical Option

Most grammar guides stop at three punctuation choices. Square brackets are the fourth — and for writers working in journalism, academia, or legal drafting, they're non-negotiable.

Primary uses:

  • Editorial insertions into quotations: When you need to clarify or complete meaning inside someone else's words without altering the quote. "She said [the board's decision] was final." The bracketed phrase was not in the original; brackets signal the writer added it for clarity.
  • [Sic]: Flags an error that exists in the original source, confirming you've reproduced it accurately. "The memo stated the project was 'compleat [sic] by Friday.'"
  • Nested parentheticals: When parentheses are already in use and a second level is needed. "The findings (published in the 2021 report [see Appendix B]) were contested."
  • Stage directions and scripts: Bracketed directions inside theatrical text.

⚠️ The key distinction: parentheses add the writer's own secondary information; square brackets signal that what's inside does not belong to the original text being quoted. Use the wrong mark and you imply the source said something they didn't.

How to Identify a Parenthetical Element: The Removability Test

Before choosing punctuation, confirm the element is actually parenthetical.

Step 1: Read the sentence without the element.
Step 2: Ask: Is the core meaning still intact? Is the grammar still complete?
Step 3: If yes — it's parenthetical. If no — the element is essential and should not be set off.

"Students who study consistently perform better." Remove who study consistently → "Students perform better." Vague and inaccurate. This is a restrictive clause — essential, no commas.
"My sister, who studies consistently, performs well." Remove who studies consistently → "My sister performs well." Still accurate. Nonrestrictive, parenthetical — commas required.

The distinction between restrictive and nonrestrictive elements is the foundation of all parenthetical punctuation. For a deeper treatment of that distinction, see our analysis on restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses.

🎯 Short Rule

If removing the element changes the sentence's core meaning, it is not parenthetical. No set-off punctuation belongs there.

Types of Parenthetical Expressions (With Punctuation Rules)

Transitional and Interrupter Words

Words and phrases like however, therefore, of course, incidentally, in fact, and nevertheless are classic interrupters. They connect ideas but don't carry essential meaning.

  • At the start: "However, the results were inconclusive." (Comma after)
  • In the middle: "The results, however, were inconclusive." (Commas both sides)
  • At the end: "The results were inconclusive, however." (Comma before)

Single-Word Interrupters: When Commas Are Optional

Here's the gray zone most guides avoid: short single-word parentheticals — too, also, indeed, perhaps, though, frankly — don't always require commas, and style guides genuinely disagree.

"I, too, was surprised." (formal, marked) vs. "I too was surprised." (informal, widely accepted)
"She perhaps didn't understand." vs. "She, perhaps, didn't understand."
"It was, frankly, a poor decision." vs. "It was frankly a poor decision."

The practical rule:

  • CMOS allows comma omission with short, lightly parenthetical adverbs when the sentence reads naturally without them.
  • AP Stylebook tends toward minimal punctuation — if the sentence flows clearly without the comma, omit it.
💡 When in doubt, read aloud.

If the pause feels natural, include the comma. If it feels clipped or awkward, drop it.

The commas are never wrong around these words. They're optional — and omitting them is increasingly accepted in contemporary editorial style.

Appositives

An appositive renames or reidentifies the noun before it. When it's nonessential, it's parenthetical.

"Her mentor, a retired editor from London, reviewed the manuscript." A retired editor from London identifies who the mentor is — but since her mentor already points to one person, the appositive is extra information. Commas set it off.

Appositives can also take em dashes for emphasis: "Her mentor — a retired editor from London — reviewed the manuscript." Same information, stronger pause, different weight.

Nonrestrictive Relative Clauses

These begin with which, who, or whom and add detail about an already-identified noun.

"The report, which took three months to complete, was well received."

The report is already identified. The clause is bonus context — parenthetical, set off by commas. For more on when inserted commas accompany these constructions, see our baseline guide on comma rules in English.

Parenthetical Notes in Formal Writing

  • Use parentheses for abbreviations on first use: The World Health Organization (WHO).
  • Use parentheses for citations: Memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive (Bartlett, 1932).
  • Use parentheses for technical references: See Figure 3 (p. 47).
  • Use square brackets for editorial clarifications inside quoted material.

Placement Matters: Beginning, Middle, and End

The punctuation pattern shifts depending on where the interrupter sits.

Beginning: One comma after the parenthetical element. "To be honest, I wasn't expecting this outcome."

Middle: Commas (or em dashes, or parentheses) on both sides. "The decision, as expected, caused significant debate."

End: One comma before the element. "She accepted the offer, reluctantly."

⚠️ One realization I had late in my editing career: missing the second comma mid-sentence is the most common parenthetical punctuation error. Writers open the aside with the first comma and forget to close it. The sentence doesn't break, but it reads off. Always close what you open.

Placement Check

  • Beginning: one opening comma only
  • Middle: two commas, or paired em dashes, or parentheses
  • End: one comma before the element only

Parenthetical Marks and Surrounding Punctuation

Parenthetical elements don't exist in isolation — they interact with the punctuation around them, and the interaction rules aren't always obvious.

Comma After a Closing Parenthesis

When a parenthetical in parentheses is followed by a comma, the comma goes outside the closing mark — never inside.

"She approved the proposal (reluctantly), and the team moved forward." ✅
"She approved the proposal (reluctantly,) and the team moved forward." ❌

Period Placement: Inside vs. Outside Parentheses

  • If the parenthetical is embedded in a sentence, the period goes outside: "She approved the plan (reluctantly)."
  • If the entire sentence is inside parentheses, the period goes inside: (She approved the plan reluctantly.)

Em Dash and Surrounding Marks

No comma immediately before an opening em dash:

"The report — which was overdue — was filed." ✅
"The report, — which was overdue — was filed." ❌

A comma can follow a closing em dash only when the sentence structure independently requires one:

"She left — quietly, without ceremony — and never returned." ✅

Semicolons and Parenthetical Clauses

When a clause containing a parenthetical precedes a semicolon, the semicolon goes after the closing mark:

"The project ran over budget (as expected); no one was surprised." ✅

Nested Parentheticals: When Marks Coexist

Real sentences — especially in academic, legal, and technical writing — sometimes require two levels of parenthetical punctuation in the same construction. The hierarchy:

  • Parentheses inside em dashes (most common): "The CEO — who had been in the role for less than a year (appointed after a surprise resignation) — reversed the decision."
  • Square brackets inside parentheses (academic standard for nested references): "The findings (published in the 2021 annual report [see Appendix B]) were later contested."

⚠️ Avoid parentheses inside parentheses — this is a consistent error in technical and legal writing. When a second level of nesting is needed inside parentheses, use square brackets: (see Table 4 [p. 112]) not ((see Table 4, p. 112)).

When nesting appears frequently in your writing, it usually signals that the sentence is carrying more than it should. Consider whether the nested element belongs in a footnote, a separate sentence, or dropped entirely.

Parenthetical Elements in Academic and Technical Writing

In formal and academic prose, parenthetical asides serve specialized functions that go beyond the literary aside.

  • Introducing abbreviations: "The World Health Organization (WHO) released its findings last autumn." After first use, the abbreviation stands alone. The parentheses are a one-time gloss.
  • Citations: "Memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive (Bartlett, 1932)." The citation is supplementary — it doesn't change the claim, it supports it. Parentheses keep it out of the sentence flow.
  • Technical notes, dates, and figure labels: "See Figure 3 (p. 47) for a complete breakdown."
📌 Use Parentheses When the Detail Is:
  • A first-use abbreviation
  • A citation or source reference
  • A page number or figure label
  • A brief technical note that only specialist readers need

Parenthetical Elements in Lists and Structured Content

The comma-based parenthetical system applies to prose sentences. In lists, bullet points, and structured formats, the rules adapt.

In bulleted lists, a parenthetical aside inside a bullet still uses parentheses or em dashes — but the surrounding comma logic no longer applies because no continuous sentence exists to interrupt.

API (Application Programming Interface) — connects software applications ✅

Commas are not used to set off the parenthetical here; the visual structure does that work.

In numbered technical lists, citation parentheticals follow the same placement conventions as in prose — outside the closing punctuation of each list item.

In tables and structured data, parenthetical information typically goes into its own cell or column rather than being nested inside another cell's content.

💡 The practical note:

When transitioning from prose to structured content, parentheses remain the default container for asides. Comma-based parentheticals belong only in continuous running text.

Parentheticals in Creative and Literary Writing

Fiction writers and essayists use parenthetical punctuation as a rhythm and voice tool — not just a grammatical one. Understanding the difference between stylistic intent and careless punctuation is what separates a deliberate technique from an error.

"She opened the letter — slowly, as if she already knew — and sat down without a word." The dashes don't just set off the aside; they build the pause into the scene's pacing.
"He was, apparently, the most qualified candidate (a fact the committee seemed eager to overlook)." The parenthetical creates a conspiratorial whisper between writer and reader.

Deliberate overloading is a known literary technique. Writers like David Foster Wallace and Joan Didion used heavy parenthetical nesting as a signature of voice — not as a failure of editing. The difference between their usage and careless stacking is that every inserted element in their work is purposeful and controlled.

🎯 The test for creative writing:

If removing the parenthetical changes the sentence's tone, rhythm, or intimacy — not just its information — it's doing stylistic work. That's intentional. If it's just clutter, cut it.

The Gray Zone: When Parenthetical Information Should Become Main Text

Not every aside belongs in punctuation brackets. Sometimes inserted material is parenthetical in form but essential in meaning — and keeping it as an aside misleads the reader about its importance.

Before (over-parenthetical): "The committee — after months of internal disagreement, three revisions to the original proposal, and two rounds of external feedback — finally approved the plan."

After (promoted to main text): "After months of internal disagreement, three proposal revisions, and two rounds of external feedback, the committee finally approved the plan."

The information inside the em dashes was substantive. Burying it in an aside obscured its significance. The rewrite gives it the weight it deserves.

📌 The rule I follow:

If removing the element changes how a reader understands the sentence's significance — not just its detail — it shouldn't be parenthetical at all. Make it its own clause or sentence.

Rewrite Test

If the aside is long, loaded, or central to your argument: pull it out, rewrite the sentence, and let it stand on its own.

Common Mistakes Writers Make

  • Using different punctuation for the same type of aside — choose one system and stay consistent within a document.
  • Forgetting the closing comma mid-sentence — parenthetical elements need a pair when embedded.
  • Using parentheses for important ideas — if it matters to your argument, integrate it or give it its own sentence.
  • Overusing em dashes — they add force; using them for every aside dilutes the effect.
  • Treating restrictive information as parenthetical — if removal breaks meaning, no set-off punctuation belongs there.
  • Using parentheses inside parentheses — use square brackets for the inner level instead.
  • Misplacing punctuation after closing marks — commas and periods go outside parentheses when the parenthetical is embedded in a sentence.
  • Overloading a sentence with multiple asides — that usually signals a rewrite, not more punctuation.

FAQ

What is the difference between an em dash and a comma when used for a parenthetical element?

Both set off a nonessential aside, but they send different signals to the reader. A comma integrates the interruption smoothly — it reads as routine context. An em dash creates a sharper pause and draws attention to what's inside it. Use commas when the aside should feel like part of the sentence's natural flow. Use em dashes when the inserted content is emphatic, surprising, or important enough to notice. The choice is tonal, not grammatical.

Do commas go inside or outside closing parentheses?

Outside, always — when the parenthetical is embedded in a sentence. The comma belongs to the surrounding sentence, not to the content inside the parentheses: "She approved the proposal (reluctantly), and the team moved forward." The period follows the same logic: if the entire sentence is inside parentheses, the period goes inside; if the parenthetical is embedded in a larger sentence, the period goes outside the closing mark.

What is the difference between parentheses and square brackets?

Parentheses add the writer's own supplementary information to a sentence — abbreviations, citations, secondary notes. Square brackets signal that what's inside does not belong to the surrounding text or original quotation — they mark editorial insertions, clarifications added to someone else's words, and [sic] notations. Using parentheses where brackets are required implies the source said something they didn't.

When should a single-word interrupter like "too," "also," or "perhaps" take commas?

It depends on the style guide and how emphatic the pause needs to be. Formally, commas around single-word interrupters are always correct: "I, too, was surprised." In contemporary editorial style (especially AP), the commas are often omitted when the sentence reads naturally without them: "I too was surprised." Read the sentence aloud — if the pause feels natural, include the commas. If they feel clipped and artificial, leave them out. Both forms are defensible.

Can you use more than one type of parenthetical punctuation in the same sentence?

Yes, and the hierarchy matters. Parentheses can appear inside em dashes: "The CEO — who had been in the role for less than a year (appointed after a surprise resignation) — reversed the decision." Square brackets go inside parentheses when a second level of nesting is needed: (see Table 4 [p. 112]). What to avoid: parentheses inside parentheses — use square brackets for the inner level instead. When multiple layers appear frequently, the sentence is usually carrying more than it should; consider restructuring.

The mark of a precise writer isn't knowing the names of every grammatical category. It's knowing what to include, what to set aside, and which punctuation signal best tells the reader how much weight to give it. Commas say "this is part of the flow." Em dashes say "pay attention to this." Parentheses say "this is here if you need it." Square brackets say "I added this — it wasn't in the original." Choose the right container and your sentences stop feeling cluttered — they start feeling controlled.
